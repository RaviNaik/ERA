How Does Attention
Work Now?

In 2017, a token could sit next to another token and know absolutely nothing about it. Attention fixed that in one stroke — and immediately handed back two new problems of its own. Everything that follows in this story is somebody's answer to one of those two problems, or to a problem the last answer created. Read in order, the sequence isn't a list of unrelated tricks. It's one long argument, each idea replying to the one before it — exact and unbounded, then cheaper to remember, then aware of distance, then longer-reaching, then recurrent again, then sparse again, and finally — in the story's newest chapter — willing to throw its own position scheme away entirely.

PROLOGUE

Before We Begin

A friend asks how attention actually works today. Not the 2017 version — the current one. Here's the honest, in-order answer.

Nobody sat down and designed today's attention mechanisms as a finished system. Each one is a patch on a real, specific wound the previous one left open — and each patch tore open a new one somewhere else. That's not a flaw in the story. It's the entire shape of it.

So this page is arranged the one way that actually shows that: strictly by when each idea was first published, not by what family it belongs to and not by which one is "best." A handful of these ideas are still the default almost everywhere; others were quietly replaced within a year or two. Both kinds get the same treatment — what it bought, what it gave up, and when it's actually the right call — because a mechanism with five upsides and no honest downside just hasn't been looked at closely enough yet.

Those eight tags are the threads running underneath the story — compute cost, cache memory, position, extension, recurrence, sparsity, compression, systems. Any one idea can pull on more than one thread at once, and the same thread resurfaces again and again, years apart, under a completely different name. The legend at the top of the timeline below filters by thread, if you want to follow just one of them start to finish.

CHAPTER ONE

The Mechanism Itself

Six small ideas, taken one at a time, computed live on the same four tokens throughout. Nothing here is named before it's needed.

Picture four tokens sitting in a row — The, cat, sat, down. Right now, each one is nothing more than a list of numbers, its embedding. If two completely unrelated tokens happened to produce identical embeddings, the network would have no way to tell them apart — a plain embedding says nothing about what a token is looking for, what it actually holds, or who's allowed to see it. Closing that gap, one small role at a time, is the entire rest of this chapter.

So before anything gets compared, every token's embedding is pushed through three separate, independently learned projections. Nothing exotic — three ordinary matrix multiplications — but keeping them separate matters: it lets what a token is asking about look completely different from what it's actually carrying, instead of forcing a token to be compared only against a copy of itself.

The same four tokens, split three ways

Those three rows have names: a query (what this token is looking for), a key (what it advertises about itself), and a value (what it actually hands over if chosen). Every one of them does a different job in the four steps below.

With a question and an advertisement for every token, the network needs exactly one number per pair, saying how well they match. The tool for that is almost embarrassingly ordinary: multiply matching positions together and add them up — a dot product. Line up every query against every key, and a grid falls out.

Every query, compared against every key

That grid is the attention score matrix, and it's already readable: whichever key produces the largest number for a given query is the token that query is, in effect, most interested in.

There's a subtle problem hiding in that grid already, though. As the width of each query and key grows, a plain dot product tends to grow right along with it — not because the tokens are more related, purely because there are more terms being multiplied and added. Left alone, that pushes the largest scores out to where the next step stops being able to tell a strong match from an overwhelming one apart.

The same grid, divided by √d_k

That one division is called scaling. It's the entire second step, and it exists purely so the step after it can still do its job.

One more wrinkle — specific to text generated left to right. Right now, every one of these four tokens can see every other one, including tokens that, during real generation, haven't been produced yet. Train on the whole sentence at once with no safeguard, and the third token would be allowed to peek at the answer while it's still supposed to be learning to predict it.

Flip the switch yourself

The fix: before the next step runs, force every score that looks into the future to negative infinity. That's the causal mask — and above, you can turn it off and watch exactly what breaks without it.

Even scaled and masked, these are still just raw numbers — some negative, none of them obligated to add up to anything in particular. What's needed is a way to turn one row of scores into something that behaves like a set of odds: every value positive, the whole row summing to one, so that handing more attention to one token necessarily costs some other token attention.

One row of scores, turned into a row of odds

That normalizing step is softmax, and the bars above are its literal output for this exact example.

One ingredient has been sitting on the sidelines this whole time: value. Every percentage from the last step is still just a percentage until it's actually used to combine information. Multiply each token's value vector by the weight its query assigned it, add all four together, and the result is a brand-new vector — one that, for the first time, genuinely reflects the rest of the sentence instead of just itself.

Four weights, four values, one new vector per token

That blend is the weighted sum of V — and it's the entire output of the layer. Six small ideas, and nothing about a token's meaning is private anymore.

Q × K → scores → scale → mask → softmax → weighted sum of V

That's the whole mechanism, precisely. Nowhere in it is there any notion of "this sequence is too long" — and yet, run it on a long sequence, and two very real costs appear that no single step above created by itself, but that the combination guarantees. Those two costs, next.

CHAPTER TWO

What It Costs

Comparing every token to every other token is a compute problem that grows with the square of the sequence length. Remembering every token during generation is a separate memory problem that grows in a straight line — but per conversation, never shared.

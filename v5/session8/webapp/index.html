How Does Attention
Work Now?

In 2017, a token could sit next to another token and know absolutely nothing about it. Attention fixed that in one stroke — and immediately handed back two new problems of its own. Everything that follows in this story is somebody's answer to one of those two problems, or to a problem the last answer created. Read in order, the sequence isn't a list of unrelated tricks. It's one long argument, each idea replying to the one before it — exact and unbounded, then cheaper to remember, then aware of distance, then longer-reaching, then recurrent again, then sparse again, and finally — in the story's newest chapter — willing to throw its own position scheme away entirely.

PROLOGUE

Before We Begin

A friend asks how attention actually works today. Not the 2017 version — the current one. Here's the honest, in-order answer.

Nobody sat down and designed today's attention mechanisms as a finished system. Each one is a patch on a real, specific wound the previous one left open — and each patch tore open a new one somewhere else. That's not a flaw in the story. It's the entire shape of it.

So this page is arranged the one way that actually shows that: strictly by when each idea was first published, not by what family it belongs to and not by which one is "best." A handful of these ideas are still the default almost everywhere; others were quietly replaced within a year or two. Both kinds get the same treatment — what it bought, what it gave up, and when it's actually the right call — because a mechanism with five upsides and no honest downside just hasn't been looked at closely enough yet.

Those eight tags are the threads running underneath the story — compute cost, cache memory, position, extension, recurrence, sparsity, compression, systems. Any one idea can pull on more than one thread at once, and the same thread resurfaces again and again, years apart, under a completely different name. The legend at the top of the timeline below filters by thread, if you want to follow just one of them start to finish.

CHAPTER ONE

The Mechanism Itself

Six small ideas, one at a time, each one computed live on the same five words. Nothing gets a name before you've felt why it's needed.

Take a five-word sentence: the bird fed its chicks. Every word arrives as nothing but a list of numbers. On its own, that list says nothing about what the word is looking for, what it advertises about itself, or what it would hand over if another word asked. The word its, in particular, is stuck — it has no way to reach back and grab bird. Giving every word those three abilities, one at a time, is the whole of this chapter.

💡 A picture to hold onto
Think of a room full of people. Each person whispers a question (the query), wears a name-tag describing what they know (the key), and holds a parcel of information (the value). Everyone reads every name-tag, works out whose tags best answer their own question, and walks away holding a blended copy of those people's parcels. That's one attention layer. The six steps below are just that idea, done carefully with numbers.

Before any comparison happens, each word's number-list is passed through three separate little transformations — three learned matrix multiplies. Same input, three different outputs. Keeping them separate is the point: what a word asks about can then look completely different from what it carries, so a word isn't forced to only ever match a copy of itself.

The five words, each split into a query, a key and a value

The three outputs have names: the query (what this word is looking for), the key (what it advertises), and the value (what it hands over if picked). Each one has a distinct job in the steps that follow.

Now the network needs one number for every pair of words — how well does this word's question match that word's name-tag? The tool is deliberately plain: multiply the two lists position-by-position and add it all up. That's a dot product. Do it for every query against every key and a grid of numbers falls out.

Every query (row) scored against every key (column)

That grid is the attention score matrix. It's already readable: whichever column holds the biggest number in a row is the word that row's word cares about most.

There's a quiet problem in that grid. The wider the query and key lists get, the bigger a plain dot product tends to be — not because the words are more related, just because more numbers got added together. Left alone, that makes the biggest scores so large that the next step can't tell "strong match" from "runaway match."

The same grid, every number divided by √dk

That single division is scaling. It changes none of the rankings — it just keeps the numbers in a range the next step can work with.

One rule applies only to text written left to right. Right now every word can see every other word — including ones that, during real generation, haven't been written yet. Train on the whole sentence at once with no guard and the word fed gets to peek at chicks while it's still supposed to be learning to predict it.

Turn the mask off yourself and watch the leak

The fix: force every score that looks forward in time to negative infinity before the next step runs. That's the causal mask. The toggle above lets you switch it off and see exactly what breaks.

Even scaled and masked, these are still raw numbers — some negative, not adding up to anything in particular. What's needed is to turn one row into a set of percentages: all positive, summing to 100%, so giving more attention to one word necessarily takes it from another.

Each row of scores, turned into a row of percentages

That step is softmax. The bars above are its actual output for this sentence — notice how its hands most of its attention to bird.

One ingredient has been waiting the whole time: the value parcels. Take each word's value, multiply it by the percentage the query assigned it, and add them all up. The result is a brand-new number-list for that word — the first one that actually reflects the rest of the sentence and not just itself.

Percentages × values, summed → one new vector per word

That blend is the weighted sum of V, and it's the whole output of the layer. Six small steps later, its is finally carrying bird around inside it.

Q × K → scores → scale → mask → softmax → weighted sum of V

That's the entire mechanism. Nowhere in it is there any notion of "this sequence is too long" — yet run it on a long one and two very real costs appear that no single step above caused alone, but that the combination guarantees. Those two costs are next, and they're the frame for everything after.

CHAPTER TWO

The Two Bills It Sends

Standard attention isn't something to fix — it bought exact, all-to-all access in one move. But that move sends two bills whenever the sequence gets long. This section makes both of them concrete, because they are the meters every idea in the timeline is trying to push down.

Bill 1 is compute: every word compares itself with every other word, so the number of comparisons is T×T. Bill 2 is memory: to generate text one word at a time, the model saves every earlier word's key and value so it never recomputes them — and that saved history grows in a straight line with T, once per conversation. Move the sliders and watch the two shapes pull apart.

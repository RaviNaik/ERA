Reversible LLMs
Memory, speed and batch size

A 20.1M-parameter GPT trained on 50M WikiText-103 tokens three ways: a standard transformer, a reversible midpoint network and a reversible Euler (symplectic) network. The winner was then trained at 2x, 4x and 8x the batch size. Every number on this page was measured on one NVIDIA RTX A6000 (48 GB) and is exported straight from the experiment results.

Headline: reversibility halved peak memory (9.45 → 4.61 GB) and cut stored activations 14x. The Euler variant then beat the baseline's loss (4.774 vs 4.828) and allowed a 2.8x larger batch. The price was 18-26% throughput, and the larger batches it enabled made the loss worse at a fixed 50M-token budget.

01 / 10 · THEORY

Why reversible layers save memory

Backprop needs every layer's input. A normal residual network therefore stores the activations of all L layers, so memory grows with depth. If each layer can be inverted, its inputs can be recomputed from its outputs during backward. Only the final state is stored, at the cost of one extra forward pass through the backbone.

Midpoint (paper eq. 4-5)leapfrog over depth
pℓ+1 = pℓ−1 + 2h · fℓ(pℓ)
fℓ(p) = Attn(LN₁ p) + MLP(LN₂(p + Attn(LN₁ p)))
inverse: pℓ−1 = pℓ+1 − 2h · fℓ(pℓ)

f is exactly the residual branch of a standard pre-LN block, so no part of the network has to be inverted analytically. Used here with h = 0.25, the best of {1/9, 0.25, 0.5} in a local sweep. The paper warns that this scheme is only marginally stable.

Euler / Hamiltonian (paper eq. 8-9)two streams
qℓ = qℓ−1 + Attnℓ(LN₁ pℓ−1)
pℓ = pℓ−1 + MLPℓ(LN₂ qℓ)
inverse: pℓ−1 = pℓ − MLP(LN₂ qℓ); qℓ−1 = qℓ − Attn(LN₁ pℓ−1)

Attention updates one stream and the MLP the other. The scheme is symplectic (volume-preserving), and it carries two 256-wide residual states at no parameter cost. Both variants have exactly the baseline's 20,105,216 parameters.

💡 What happened
  • Euler started behind (+0.17 at 2.5M tokens) but overtook the baseline at ~10M tokens and finished at 4.774, 0.053 lower. It reached the baseline's final loss with 15% fewer tokens (42.5M).
  • Midpoint tracked the baseline within ±0.02 all the way and finished 0.014 higher, in line with the paper's "within ~0.01" for midpoint.
  • Per unit of wall-clock time the baseline still leads: when it finishes (5.9 min), Euler is at 4.882 and midpoint at 4.895. Euler needs 6.5 min to reach the baseline's final loss.
  • A plausible reason for Euler's gain: two residual streams give a wider residual "memory" at no parameter cost. With a single seed this is suggestive, not proven.

Stored activations are what autograd keeps between forward and backward. The peak also includes the transient working set of backward, which reversibility shrinks much less.

💡 Takeaway
  • The baseline stores 41.8 MB per 512-token sequence and adds 37.7 MB per layer (batch 8). Both reversible stacks store 3.0 MB per sequence and a constant 74 MB from 3 to 24 layers.
  • Peak memory falls 51% (9.45 → 4.61 GB), less than the 14x cut in stored activations, because backward still materializes one layer's reconstruction and the float64 streams and their gradients.
06 / 10 · SPEED

Where the time goes, and what exact reversal costs

Mean step time per 65,536 tokens, split into phases. "Forward" includes the fused 50,257-way vocab projection and its gradient, which is 58% of the model's FLOPs and runs bandwidth-bound.

Step time per 65,536 tokens (ms)
Cost of exact reversal: one step at batch 128 (ms)
💡 Takeaway
  • Throughput: baseline 154.8k, midpoint 126.4k (−18%), Euler 115.0k tokens/s (−26%), against the ~14% extra FLOPs predicted for the recompute. MFU was 13.4%, 10.9% and 9.9% (A6000 bf16 peak: 154.8 TFLOP/s).
  • About half the slowdown is exactness. The A6000 runs fp64 at 1/32 of its fp32 rate, so exact streams add 13% (midpoint) or 26% (Euler, two quantized updates per layer). With plain fp32 streams, reversal costs only 8-10%, but it drifts (Section 2).

💡 What the scaling shows
  • Memory scaled as designed: ~16 MB per extra sequence against ~42 MB for the baseline. Batch 1024 fits in 16.4 GB, less than twice the baseline's 9.45 GB at batch 128.
  • Speed barely moved: +6.6% from batch 128 to 1024, because the GPU is already saturated at 128. Wall clock fell only from 7.7 to 7.2 min.
  • Loss got much worse: 4.77 → 5.22 → 6.17 → 6.75. This is the critical batch size effect: at 20M parameters and this early in training, 65k tokens per step is already enough, so fewer and larger steps waste tokens. sqrt LR scaling added gradient spikes (max norm 257 at 8x) rather than progress.
09 / 10 · THE MAXIMUM

Largest batch that fits: 896 vs 2528

Doubling until out-of-memory, then bisecting to 32 sequences, with real training steps on the 48 GB A6000. This is the equivalent of the paper's Table 3.

Peak memory vs batch (✕ = out of memory)
💡 Why 2.8x and not the paper's ~10x
  • The peak slope sets the limit, not the stored activations. Stored activations are 14x smaller, but the reversible peak still grows ~16 MB per sequence (reconstruction working set plus float64 streams and gradients) against ~42 MB for the baseline. That 2.6x ratio matches the measured 2.8x.
  • Only 9 layers. The savings grow with depth; the paper's largest gains are on much deeper models.
  • 64% of the parameters are the 50k-vocab embedding and head, whose loss buffers are identical in both architectures.
